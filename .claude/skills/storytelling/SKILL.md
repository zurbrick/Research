# Storytelling

A toolkit for turning flat, feature-listing, or jargon-heavy content into a narrative that grips an audience and moves them to act. Built around 13 techniques drawn from high-performing communicators.

## When to use this skill

Reach for it whenever the goal is to **persuade, inspire, or be remembered** — not just to inform. Typical triggers:

- "Tell a story about…", "make this compelling", "hook the reader", "make it punchy"
- Writing or revising a pitch, talk, keynote, demo script, or webinar
- A presentation, slide deck, or fundraising deck
- A landing page, launch post, announcement, or marketing email
- A blog post intro, README opener, or research narrative that needs to land
- A personal essay, bio, or "about" section

If the user only wants dry reference docs, API specs, or a bug fix, this skill is overkill — skip it.

## How to use it

1. **Clarify the essentials first** (ask only if unknown): Who is the *audience*? What single *action or feeling* do you want from them at the end? What is the *one idea* they must remember?
2. **Pick the spine**, then layer techniques — don't force all 13 into every piece. A short post might use 3–4; a keynote might use all of them.
3. **Open and close deliberately.** The hook (#1) and the inspirational close (#2) carry disproportionate weight. Draft them first.
4. **Read it aloud.** Cut anything that doesn't earn its place. Then apply the checklist below.

## The 13 techniques

### 1. Start with a powerful hook
Open with something the audience can't ignore — a surprising fact, a sharp question, a vivid moment, or a bold claim. Never warm up; start in motion. The first sentence decides whether they read the second.

### 2. Close with inspiration
End by leaving the audience inspired and motivated to take action. The last line is what they carry out the door — make it a call forward, not a summary.

### 3. Connect to a larger vision
Your offer should serve a bigger mission. Tie the specific thing (a product, a finding, a feature) to a larger change in the world the audience wants to be part of. Sell the "why it matters," not just the "what."

### 4. Create an experience
Make the audience feel like they're part of something special — not spectators but participants. Use "you," invite them into a scene, give them a moment to inhabit.

### 5. Highlight the problem
Identify the audience's real pain points and make them vivid before offering a solution. Build tension and the desire for relief. People don't move until they feel the problem.

### 6. Make it personal
Let the audience see the human behind the work. A real anecdote, a stake, a vulnerability, a moment of doubt. Specificity and honesty build trust faster than polish.

### 7. Use strategic repetition
Repeat your key message creatively, not robotically — vary the wording, return to a motif, plant a phrase and bring it back. Repetition is how a message becomes memorable.

### 8. Show, don't tell
Let the audience experience the point instead of being told it. Replace adjectives ("it's fast") with demonstrations, examples, numbers, and concrete images ("it loaded before you finished blinking"). Bring ideas to life.

### 9. Practice relentlessly
Great storytelling looks effortless but is the result of deliberate practice. Draft, cut, rehearse, and revise. Effortlessness is earned — assume the first version is not the final one.

### 10. Create a villain
Paint a picture of what's broken in the industry, the status quo, or the old way of doing things. Give the audience something to root against — a clear antagonist makes the hero (and the solution) matter.

### 11. Master the pause
Don't rush. Give the audience time to absorb the message. In writing this is white space, a short line, a paragraph break; in speech it's silence. The pause is a tool — silence lets a point land.

### 12. Build anticipation
Make the audience crave what comes next. Tease, foreshadow, open a loop you'll close later. Leave them leaning forward and wanting more.

### 13. Simplify complex ideas
If you can't explain your idea to a 5-year-old, simplify it until you can. Strip jargon, use analogy, find the one-sentence version. Clarity is the price of being understood.

## Narrative spines to build on

Most strong pieces follow one of these arcs. Pick one as scaffolding, then decorate with the techniques.

- **Problem → Agitate → Solution (PAS):** name the pain (#5), make it ache (#10, #11), reveal the fix (#8). Great for landing pages and pitches.
- **Hero's journey (compressed):** an ordinary person, a call, a struggle against a villain (#10), a transformation. Great for case studies and founder stories.
- **Before → After → Bridge:** the world today, the better world (#3), and the path between. Great for vision talks and launches.
- **Spark → Stakes → Payoff:** a hook (#1), rising tension and anticipation (#12), an inspiring resolution (#2). Great for talks and demos.

## Quality checklist

Before calling a piece done, confirm:

- [ ] The first sentence earns the second (hook lands).
- [ ] There is one clear idea the audience will remember.
- [ ] A real problem is felt before any solution appears.
- [ ] At least one moment is *shown*, not told (example, image, or number).
- [ ] The specific thing is connected to a larger why.
- [ ] There's a human, personal element — not just abstractions.
- [ ] Jargon is stripped or explained in plain language.
- [ ] The close points forward and prompts action.
- [ ] It's been tightened at least once — no filler survives.

## Example: before & after

**Before (flat, feature-listing):**
> Our library provides a vectorized backtesting engine with support for multiple asset classes and a pluggable data layer. It is fast and easy to use.

**After (with techniques applied):**
> Last year a strategy I believed in lost three months of work to a single look-ahead bug I never saw. *(#6 personal, #5 problem)* Most backtesters let that happen — they trust your code to be honest about time. *(#10 villain)* Ours doesn't. *(#11 pause)* Point it at ten years of data and it replays the market tick by tick, exactly as you would have lived it — no peeking, no do-overs. *(#8 show)* You stop debugging your past and start trusting your edge. *(#3 vision, #2 close)*

The "after" isn't longer for its own sake — every added line does narrative work, and each is tagged with the technique it uses.
